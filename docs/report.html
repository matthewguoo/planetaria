Gating Post-Earnings Announcement Drift with a Large Language Model

A five-year replication with direct training-contamination controls and a mechanical lookahead audit.
Universe after-close earnings events, · model , effort · of inference · revised 2026-08-06.
Abstract

1Motivation

Post-earnings announcement drift — the tendency of prices to keep moving in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeks after the announcement — is among the oldest and most replicated anomalies in the literature.[1][2][3] Its usual formulation is slow: sort on standardised unexpected earnings, hold for sixty days. The version tested here is the opposite end of the horizon. The release crosses after the close, the tape reprices within the minute, and the question is only whether the first move continues into the next session.

The mechanical form of that trade states in one line: if the after-hours reaction exceeds a threshold, enter in its direction and exit at the next session's close. On its own it is unreliable — it makes money in trending regimes and loses it in stressed ones. The hypothesis under test is that a language model reading the release text can tell those cases apart at announcement time, before the outcome is known: that the release itself carries information about whether the tape's initial verdict will hold.

This extends a long line of work showing that the textual content of financial disclosure predicts returns beyond the numbers,[6][7][8] and a newer line applying instruction-tuned language models to the same question.[9] It also inherits that newer line's central difficulty. A model trained on text written after these events may recall them, and a recalled outcome is not a prediction.[10][11][12] Section 4 describes what was done about it; Section 7 describes what could not be.

What changed in this revision

Two defects were found after the first draft and both moved the numbers. The cached price panels exited on the last session of a four-calendar-day fetch window rather than the next one, so every figure labelled T+1 in the first draft was really a two-to-four session hold. And an EDGAR timestamp was read as Eastern time when it is UTC, admitting midday filers into an after-close sample (Section 4.4). Every number here is rebuilt on per-session exits and on the events that survive the timing audit. Nothing in this paper is transcribed by hand: the page renders from the same JSON the harness emits.

Two details matter for reading everything that follows. The gate is an agreement rule: the model's direction must match the sign of the tape's reaction, and any disagreement is a no-trade. And the bracket is a vol-scaled stop with a target at twice its distance, which the study measures separately from the raw round trip because the two answer different questions — whether the gate picks direction, and what the strategy as configured would have returned.

3Data and universe

The universe is built from EDGAR item 2.02 filings for the thousand most liquid US issuers, ranked on the first sessions of each window so that ranking never sees future liquidity. Prices are split- and dividend-adjusted SIP data. The entry is the last after-hours print in the fifteen minutes following 16:05 ET; the exit is re-derived per session from minute bars.

4Method

4.1 One request per event

Each event is a single self-contained request: no conversation history, no shared session, no cross-event state. The task line names the ticker, states that consensus is unknown, gives the five-session run-up into the print, and asks for a structured verdict — EPS and revenue against consensus, guidance direction, quality flags, a direction, a confidence, and one line of prose. Output is schema-constrained, so there is one JSON object and no tool-call channel. The tape reaction is not in the prompt. The model never sees the move it is being asked to gate.

Consensus reads unknown in every prompt because that is what the live path sees for roughly a third of names — the free data tier keeps no history. This is a disclosed deviation from live, and it understates rather than flatters: live has real consensus for the other two thirds.

4.3 Contamination controls

The model's corpus runs to roughly 2026-05, so four of these five years are in-corpus by construction and cutoff-slicing would discard the study. The controls therefore measure the contamination channel rather than avoid it. Three are reported here; two more were designed and not run, and Section 7 says so.

The first is the permutation null. Verdict labels are shuffled within calendar month — preserving both the monthly mix of verdicts and the monthly return regime — and the gate re-scored a thousand times. This gives an exact null for "verdicts carry no event-specific information" with no distributional assumption.

The second is the contamination gradient. Memorisation should not be flat in time: older events are more discussed and more repeated in any corpus, so a memorised edge should rise with event age. The gated return is regressed on days since the event.

The third is no retrieval. The requests declare no tools, and reading the returned messages back off the Batch API finds no non-text content block and no tool-use stop reason across every response. The model could not look the answer up.

4.4 The lookahead audit

The load-bearing claim of a study like this is that every input was available at decision time. Reading the code and concluding that it looks right is not evidence, so seven checks were written to run mechanically. Where a property can be stated as an invariance — the output does not change when the future changes — it is tested that way, because a perturbation proof cannot be fooled by a variable that merely looks innocuous.

Check 2 failed

EDGAR's acceptanceDateTime was read as Eastern time carrying a spurious Z. It is real UTC. Two facts settle it, both reproducible from the cached submissions: the median post-15:00 acceptance shifts 58 minutes between April–October and November–February, which is a daylight-saving boundary and not something issuers do; and read as Eastern, the histogram of 68,394 filings puts submissions at midnight and 02:00, when EDGAR is closed.

The calendar builder classifies an event as after-close by slicing that string and comparing to 16:00, so amc has meant "accepted after 16:00 UTC" — after noon Eastern in summer. Of scored events, were accepted before the closing bell and are not after-hours releases at all, and were accepted after the 16:20 entry, so the release was not public at the price the study enters at.

EDGAR acceptance time, converted to Eastern

Restating the study on each bucket separates the question. Removing the mis-timed events improves the result, which is the reassuring direction: they were diluting it, not creating it. The gate is anti-predictive on the pre-close bucket, exactly as it should be when the measured "reaction" is not a reaction to anything.

The correction is a filter, not a repair, and it is worth being precise about what the underlying defect is. The reaction window is hard-coded to the fifteen minutes after 16:05 rather than keyed to each release's own acceptance time. For a filer that crosses at 16:22 the measured "reaction" is therefore not a reaction to anything — it is the tape before the news — and the only sound response available without refetching every price path is to drop the event. The principled repair is to measure each reaction in the fifteen minutes following that event's acceptance, which would recover the events accepted between 16:21 and 17:30 rather than discarding them. It would also change every entry price in the study, so it is not attempted here.

The cost of the hard cliff is concrete: NVIDIA's three qualifying events were accepted at 16:21 and 16:22 and are excluded by one and two minutes. Separately, the per-day top-five was ranked over a candidate pool that still contained the mis-timed names, so selection on affected days is not what a corrected calendar would produce. Fixing that would change the universe and force the study to be re-scored.

6What the evidence supports

7Limitations

  • Two contamination arms were designed and not run. An identity-ablation arm (ticker, company name, exchange tags, URLs and every date scrubbed, with the model asked to name the company it thinks it is reading, so the re-identification rate is measured rather than assumed) and a memory-probe arm (ticker and date only, no release text, so any edge is pure recall). Until they run, the evidence against memorisation is indirect: the permutation null, the flat age gradient, and the absence of a retrieval channel.
  • Regime priors survive any scrub. A model trained on text written after these events knows what kind of company this is even when it recalls no specific release. The ablation arm would bound this; nothing removes it.
  • Survivorship is structural. The universe is built from today's SEC ticker map, so an issuer fully delisted since could never have entered it. The count of missing names is therefore zero by construction, which is not a clean bill of health — it means the bias is in the map rather than the filter, and cannot be measured from these artefacts. It flatters the bear regime most.
  • Costs are a flat 13bp round trip, not a per-name spread model. The fade mutations are the most exposed to this, since fading a 5% move means crossing the after-hours book in the direction nobody wants; the cost sensitivity in the mutation study shows the fade surviving to 100bp, but a flat assumption is still a flat assumption.
  • The after-hours fill is unproven. The entry assumes a fill at the reaction print. The live account is not entitled to the consolidated feed, so no after-hours entry has ever filled — the part of this strategy least supported by evidence is the part that puts the trade on.
  • One bear market. 2022 is the only stressed regime in the sample, and it is where the mechanical baseline loses most and the gate earns most of its spread. A second one would be worth more than another year of trend.
  • The reaction window is not keyed to acceptance. It is a fixed 16:05–16:20 slice, so a release that crosses at 16:22 is dropped rather than measured from its own timestamp — NVIDIA's three qualifying events are excluded by one and two minutes. The timing filter also removes contaminated events without undoing their effect on the per-day liquidity ranking (Section 4.4).
  • Every fill is paper. No result here has been traded.
