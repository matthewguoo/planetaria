Gating Post-Earnings Announcement Drift with a Large Language Model

Can a language model tell which earnings reactions continue — and is it reading, or remembering?
after-close earnings events, · and three earlier models · revised 2026-08-06.
Abstract

1 What is being tested

When a company reports earnings after the closing bell, its shares reprice in the thin after-hours market within seconds. By the time anyone has finished reading the release, the move has already happened. That first move is not the opportunity. The question this paper asks is narrower and harder: does the first move continue into the next trading day, or does it reverse?

There is good reason to think it continues. Post-earnings announcement drift — the tendency of prices to keep moving in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeks afterwards — is among the oldest and most replicated anomalies in finance.[1][2][3] But the classic version of that finding is slow: rank companies by how far earnings beat expectations, then hold for sixty days. Trading it overnight is a different proposition, and simply riding every large after-hours move does not work. It pays in calm markets and loses in stressed ones.

So the strategy needs something that separates the moves that continue from the ones that reverse, and it needs it before the outcome is known. The candidate tested here is a large language model that reads the earnings release itself — the actual text the company published — and states which way it thinks the stock should go. The model is never shown the price move it is being asked to judge. When its verdict agrees with the direction the market has already taken, the trade is taken; otherwise it is skipped.

That idea has a substantial pedigree: the textual content of financial disclosure has long been shown to predict returns beyond the numbers alone,[6][7][8] and a newer literature applies instruction-tuned language models to the same question.[9] It also inherits that newer literature's central problem, which is the one this paper spends the most effort on. A model trained on text written after these earnings came out may simply remember what happened next. A remembered outcome looks exactly like a skilled prediction and is worth nothing.[10][11][12] Section 4 sets out the four separate controls built to detect that; Section 5.9 reports the one that settles it.

Six terms this paper uses constantly
reaction
the percentage price move in the fifteen minutes after the release crosses, measured against that day's official closing price.
the gate
the rule that decides whether to trade: take the position only when the model's stated direction agrees with the sign of the reaction. Events it takes are gated; events it declines are refused.
T+1
the holding period — enter after the close, exit at the close of the next full trading session. T+3 means three sessions.
basis point (bp)
one hundredth of one percent. 100 bp = 1%. All returns here are per trade, in basis points, net of trading costs.
selection spread
the paper's central measure of whether the model is worth anything: the average return on gated events minus the average return on refused ones. If the model carries no information, this is zero.
quoted spread
a different, unrelated quantity — the gap between the best bid and the best offer, i.e. what it costs to trade. Always written quoted spread to keep the two apart (Section 6).

Four more that appear once the results start:

never stand down
an alternative to the gate that trades every event instead of skipping some: with the tape when the model's verdict agrees with it, against the tape when it disagrees or is neutral. The gate's refusals become trades in the opposite direction rather than no trade at all (Section 5.4).
pure LLM direction
a third rule that ignores the tape entirely and trades whichever way the model called, sitting out only the neutrals. It turns out to be the same thing as never standing down on every event where the model took a side — the two differ only over the neutrals (Section 5.4).
holdout
the years 2016 to mid-2021, which were added to the study only after every design decision had already been made on the later years. Nothing about the strategy was chosen by looking at them, so performance there is a test rather than a fit (Section 5.8).
in-corpus / after cutoff
whether an earnings release happened before or after the date a given model's training data ends — that is, whether the model could possibly have read what happened next (Section 5.9).
gross exposure
how much of the account is invested at once, averaged over time. The strategy holds a few positions for one session each, so it is idle much of the time; unless stated otherwise every account figure here runs at about 30% average exposure (Section 6.3).

2 The strategy, stated exactly

The thesis in one paragraph

When a company releases earnings after the close, the tape reprices it within the minute — by the time anyone has read the release, the move has happened. What is left to trade is not the move but whether it continues into the next session. Taking every large after-hours move mechanically does not work: it pays in trending regimes and loses in stressed ones. The claim under test is that a language model reading the release text at announcement time — before the outcome is known, and without being shown the move it is judging — can tell the continuations from the reversals.

2.1 The rule, step by step

  1. 15:30 ET — freeze the watchlist. Of the companies reporting after today's close, take the most liquid by prior-session dollar volume. Snapshot each one's price, its move so far today, and its five-session run-up.
  2. 16:04 ET — re-anchor. Replace each snapshot with the post-auction price, so the reaction is measured against the official close rather than against 15:30 drift. Names that have already moved past the gate keep their original anchor — a move that size before the release means the news is already out, and re-anchoring would erase exactly the signal being traded.
  3. The release crosses. The first results-bearing document for a watched name — an EDGAR 8-K, or a single-ticker numbers headline, whichever lands first — starts the clock.
  4. Wait . The minutes right after a release are the worst microstructure of the day: price discovery, the widest spreads, dip-then-spike whipsaw. The tape must still clear the gate when the delay elapses.
  5. Read the release once. One model call on the release text, returning a fixed schema: EPS and revenue against consensus, guidance direction, quality flags, an overall direction, a confidence, and one line of prose. No tools, no conversation history, no cross-event state — and the tape's move is not in the prompt.
  6. The gate. Trade only if the model's direction agrees with the sign of the tape's move and that move is at least . Long if both are up, short if both are down. Anything else is journalled and skipped.
  7. Size by risk, not by conviction alone. Risk of equity at the stop, so notional = risk ÷ stop distance; then scale by the model's confidence, shrink on quality flags, and halve when the move is already outsized.
  8. Exit. Whichever comes first: the vol-scaled stop, the target at twice that distance, or the time stop at .

2.2 What is deliberately not traded

The gate is an agreement rule, and the refusals are most of what it does: a bullish read against a falling tape is a no-trade, and so is a neutral read against any tape. Roughly a third of qualifying events are declined on these grounds. Section 5.4 asks whether that refusal is the right use of the information, and finds it is not.

Two further vetoes fire after the gate passes: an after-hours spread cap, and a guard against shorting a name that has already been crushed into the print. Both are listed in the table below.

4 Method

4.1 One request per event

Each event is a single self-contained request: no conversation history, no shared session, no cross-event state. The task line names the ticker, states that consensus is unknown, gives the five-session run-up into the print, and asks for a structured verdict — EPS and revenue against consensus, guidance direction, quality flags, a direction, a confidence, and one line of prose. Output is schema-constrained, so there is one JSON object and no tool-call channel. The tape reaction is not in the prompt. The model never sees the move it is being asked to gate.

Consensus reads unknown in every prompt because that is what the live path sees for roughly a third of names — the free data tier keeps no history. This is a disclosed deviation from live, and it understates rather than flatters: live has real consensus for the other two thirds.

4.2 Effort calibration

Reasoning effort was calibrated before committing the budget, then re-run at scale: the model reads the identical release twice, once at low effort and once at medium, and the two verdicts are compared event by event.

4.3 Contamination controls

Claude Opus 5's training data runs to 2026-05, so almost every event in this sample is inside its corpus by construction. Simply discarding the in-corpus events would leave too little study to be worth reporting, so the controls here measure the contamination channel rather than avoid it — with one exception, described last, which avoids it outright by changing the model instead of the sample.

The first is the permutation null. Verdict labels are shuffled within calendar month — preserving both the monthly mix of verdicts and the monthly return regime — and the gate re-scored a thousand times. This gives an exact null for "verdicts carry no event-specific information" with no distributional assumption.

The second is the contamination gradient. Memorisation should not be flat in time: older events are more discussed and more repeated in any corpus, so a memorised edge should rise with event age. The gated return is regressed on days since the event.

The third is no retrieval. The requests declare no tools, and reading the returned messages back off the Batch API finds no non-text content block and no tool-use stop reason across every response. The model could not look the answer up.

The fourth is different in kind, and it is the one that carries the weight. Rather than trying to detect memorisation inside a model that has it, the out-of-training test holds the events fixed and swaps the reader: the same releases are scored by four models whose training data ends on four different dates, so an event that one model could have read about is one another could not. That converts an unobservable into a comparison, and Section 5.9 reports it. The two direct ablation arms — an identity scrub and a no-text memory probe — are reported in Section 5.7, and Section 5.9 revisits what the second of them actually measured.

4.4 The lookahead audit

The load-bearing claim of a study like this is that every input was available at decision time. Reading the code and concluding that it looks right is not evidence, so seven checks were written to run mechanically. Where a property can be stated as an invariance — the output does not change when the future changes — it is tested that way, because a perturbation proof cannot be fooled by a variable that merely looks innocuous.

One thing a replication will get wrong

EDGAR's acceptanceDateTime ends in a Z and looks like Eastern time carrying a spurious suffix. It is real UTC, and reading it as Eastern moves every filing four or five hours, which is enough to turn a midday filer into an after-close one. Two facts settle it, both reproducible from the cached submissions: the median post-15:00 acceptance shifts 58 minutes between April–October and November–February, which is a daylight-saving boundary and not something issuers do; and read as Eastern, the histogram of every cached filing puts submissions at midnight and 02:00, when EDGAR is closed.

Every timestamp in this study is therefore converted from UTC to Eastern before use, and each event's reaction window is keyed to its own converted stamp rather than to a fixed hour of the clock. Of scored events, are accepted before the closing bell and more than twenty minutes after it — a spread wide enough that no single fixed window could measure them all.

EDGAR acceptance time, converted to Eastern

4.5 How the reaction is measured

Each reaction is measured in the fifteen minutes following that event's own acceptance stamp, with the anchor left at the 16:05 post-auction print so that move_pct means the same thing here as it does in the live engine. Keying the window to the release rather than to the clock is what lets a company that files at 16:35 be measured at all, and it removes any need for a timing filter: an event cannot be misclassified as after-close when its classification is its own timestamp.

5.9 Reading or remembering? A test with four models

Everything above shares one weakness, and it is the one that matters most. Almost every event in this panel happened before the model's training data was collected. If the model has read a news article describing what the stock did the next day, then a "prediction" is a lookup, and this paper measures nothing at all. Section 5.7's arms attack that from inside a single model, and neither settles it: the identity scrub leaks, because the model recognises the company anyway, and the memory probe gives an upper bound without saying how much of the real edge it explains.

There is a cleaner test available, and it costs nothing but inference. Anthropic publishes a training-data cutoff for each model, and the cutoffs differ across the Opus family. An earnings release from March 2026 falls inside Claude Opus 5's corpus and outside Claude Opus 4.8's. Run every model on the same releases and the confound turns into an experiment: hold the event fixed, vary only whether the model could have memorised it, and whatever difference remains is the memorisation effect.

The estimate

The cells above mix two things a reader should not have to disentangle by eye: some models are better than others, and some events are easier than others. The estimator below removes both. It is a two-way fixed-effects regression of the per-trade return on a single indicator — was this event inside this model's training data — with a separate intercept for every event and every model. The event intercepts absorb the news, the size of the move and the market that week; the model intercepts absorb general ability. What is left is identified purely from disagreement within a single event between models on opposite sides of a cutoff. Standard errors cluster by event, because four verdicts on one release are not four independent observations.

Table 20: Memorisation effect, naive and with event and model intercepts

Why this reports an equivalence test

A non-significant coefficient is not evidence that memorisation is absent — it is equally consistent with a test too small to detect it, and reporting it as reassurance would be the same error the rest of this paper spends its length trying to avoid. The claim wanted here is a positive one, so the result is stated as a TOST equivalence test: the effect is bounded within ± at 90% confidence. That bound is what should be compared against the the gate earns on the same events.

6 What the fills actually cost

Every return in this paper is net of a flat round trip. That number was an assumption, and it is the most load-bearing one in the study: the gated edge is around per trade, so an assumption wrong by a hundred basis points changes the conclusion and one wrong by three hundred reverses it. This section replaces the assumption with a measurement.

The instrument is the SIP national best bid and offer, read at the exact minute each event enters and again at the minute it exits. Nothing here is modelled; the quoted spread is what the book actually showed.

6.2 Randomised fills

A fill is not the midpoint of whichever quote happened to be standing. To put a band around that, the realised spread for every event is redrawn from the empirical distribution of measured spreads in the same liquidity and move-size bucket, and the whole book is repriced — times. Bootstrapping the observed distribution rather than fitting a normal to it is deliberate: earnings-night spreads are violently right-skewed, and a Gaussian would quietly delete the tail that does the damage.

8 Limitations

  • Two contamination arms were designed and not run. An identity-ablation arm (ticker, company name, exchange tags, URLs and every date scrubbed, with the model asked to name the company it thinks it is reading, so the re-identification rate is measured rather than assumed) and a memory-probe arm (ticker and date only, no release text, so any edge is pure recall). Until they run, the evidence against memorisation is indirect: the permutation null, the flat age gradient, and the absence of a retrieval channel.
  • Regime priors survive any scrub. A model trained on text written after these events knows what kind of company this is even when it recalls no specific release. The ablation arm would bound this; nothing removes it.
  • Survivorship is structural. The universe is built from today's SEC ticker map, so an issuer fully delisted since could never have entered it. The count of missing names is therefore zero by construction, which is not a clean bill of health — it means the bias is in the map rather than the filter, and cannot be measured from these artefacts. It flatters the bear regime most.
  • Costs are a flat round trip, not a per-name spread model. The fade mutations are the most exposed to this, since fading a move means crossing the after-hours book in the direction nobody wants; the cost sensitivity in the mutation study shows the fade surviving to 100bp, but a flat assumption is still a flat assumption.
  • The after-hours fill is unproven. The entry assumes a fill at the reaction print. The live account is not entitled to the consolidated feed, so no after-hours entry has ever filled — the part of this strategy least supported by evidence is the part that puts the trade on.
  • Few stressed regimes. Drawdowns are where the mechanical baseline loses most and the gate earns most of its spread, so the number of them in the sample bounds how much can be claimed.
  • The reaction window is not keyed to acceptance. It is a fixed 16:05–16:20 slice, so a release that crosses at 16:22 is dropped rather than measured from its own timestamp — NVIDIA's three qualifying events are excluded by one and two minutes. The timing filter also removes contaminated events without undoing their effect on the per-day liquidity ranking (Section 4.4).
  • Every fill is paper. No result here has been traded.
